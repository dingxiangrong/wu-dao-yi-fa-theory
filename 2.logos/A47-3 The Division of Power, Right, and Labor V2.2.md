# A47-3 The Division of Power, Right, and Labor V2.2
# Abstract

This paper is the special research document _Divergence of Power, Right and Labor_ in the A47 series. It defines fundamental concepts including asset, capital, asset allocation and asset transaction, establishes a tripartite classification framework for private capital, corporate capital and sovereign capital, distinguishes two capital appreciation paths: real operational appreciation and financial spread-based appreciation, and derives four basic inferences for capital expansion. Drawing on authoritative time-series datasets such as the Federal Reserve Z.1, DFA, BLS and EPI, it observes empirical facts from 1989 to 2025: the continuous concentration of U.S. private capital among top households, the long-term lag of labor compensation growth behind productivity gains, and the falling labor share of national income. It explains the formation mechanism of the capital-labor scissors gap, and sorts out the transmission chain: distribution imbalance → household leverage ceiling → passive sovereign liability backstop. It compares America's passive sovereign liability backstop model with Norway's proactive sovereign capital hedging model, and analyzes two primary sources of principal for sovereign capital: earmarked capital gains tax and revenue from nationally owned natural resources. It further deduces America's structural predicament: the historical windows to build sovereign capital via natural resource dividends and capital gains tax have largely closed. The country has to rely on sovereign debt to postpone distributional contradictions, forming a self-reinforcing debt expansion cycle. Coupled with distorted income statistics, high livelihood costs and sustained pressure on grassroots households, the room for mild incremental reforms keeps shrinking. This paper only conducts mechanism deduction and empirical comparison instead of making deterministic predictions of crises. It provides underlying economic mechanism support for the institutional reconstruction proposals within the A47 series.

# Chapter 1 Fundamental Conceptual Definitions

> **Note**: This chapter provides the foundational conceptual definitions for the entire text. All subsequent derivation, argumentation, and conclusions reuse these concepts without re-defining them.
## 1.1 Assets

**Asset**: An economic rights-bearing vehicle capable of generating cash flow.

**Characteristics of assets**: Assets belong to the category of objects, are not bound to any specific subject, possess no will and no drive for expansion or profit-seeking. They serve as tools, means, and vehicles of economic operation, and as such lack the capacity to act on their own.

**Assets are divided into three forms**:
- Monetary assets: bank deposits, etc.
- Financial assets: equity, bonds, funds, loans extended to others, and various securities certificates and financial contracts.
- Non-financial assets: productive physical assets such as plant, equipment, production lines, and inventory.
 
> **Note 1** [Example]: Bank deposits, funds lent to external parties, and productive plant are all assets; cash kept at home possesses only a payment function and generates no cash flow, and therefore is not an asset.
> 
> **Note 2** [Conceptual distinction]: The concepts defined in this text differ from the statistical account concepts of traditional accounting; accounting standards list cash on hand as an asset, whereas this text adopts cash-flow-generating capacity as its criterion of judgment.
## 1.2 Capital

**Capital**: Assets that belong to a given subject and are used for the purpose of value appreciation.

**Characteristics of capital**: Capital possesses subjectivity; this subjectivity manifests as agency, exhibiting behavioral tendencies toward circulation and the pursuit of scale growth — the embodiment of capital's profit-seeking nature.
 
> **Note 1** [Example]: A property leased out for rental income is capital; a self-occupied residence serves only consumption, and even if the title belongs to an individual, it does not constitute capital.
> 
> **Note 2** [Conceptual distinction]: Capital is necessarily an asset, but an asset is not necessarily capital; only when an asset is deployed by a subject in value-appreciation activities does it transform into capital.
### 1.2.1 Classification of Capital-Holding Subjects

In real society, according to the subject holding capital, three categories exist:
- **Private capital**: Capital whose ultimate beneficiaries are natural-person households.
- **Corporate capital**: Operating and financial assets held by legal persons with the aim of value appreciation.
- **Sovereign capital**: Public capital held by sovereign public sectors, as opposed to private capital.

As a legal vehicle, the legal person is not itself the ultimate beneficiary of capital. Tracing upward along the shareholding structure, the underlying equity interests of corporate capital ultimately map to actual controllers, shareholders, and other private subjects, and belong in substance to private capital. In other words, private capital is the terminal node at the apex of the entire control chain, while corporate capital is the legal shell of the same private capital at intermediate points of the control chain.

> **Limitation of statistical coverage**: Mainstream statistics such as the Federal Reserve's Z.1 Flow of Funds and the Distributional Financial Accounts (DFA) count only assets directly registered in the name of households, without performing look-through tracing of beneficial ownership. Legal persons in the United States hold a vast amount of financial assets (over USD 200 trillion), the underlying interests of which in essence belong to private capital but are not included in the household-sector statistical scope. Public data therefore systematically underestimates the true total scale and degree of concentration of private capital.
### 1.2.2 The Policy Control Channel Between Corporate Capital and Private Capital

Corporate capital and private capital are not completely sealed off from each other; a channel of mobility exists between them, with tax rules serving as the core constraint gate.
When capital moves between corporate vehicles and private subjects — such as dividends, equity transfers, asset dispositions, and profit withdrawals — income tax and capital gains tax are triggered, thereby regulating the pace and cost of capital flows between the two vehicles. Capital holders may rely on corporate vehicles to hold assets long-term and defer taxation; however, for capital to be realized from the corporate to the private end, it must pass through this fiscal-and-tax control mechanism.
### 1.2.3 Classification Principle for Custodial-Held Equity Assets

**Custodial-held equity assets**: Asset forms in which capital interests are registered under the name of third-party custodial vehicles (funds, trusts, pension plans, sovereign funds, etc.) rather than directly under the name of the ultimate beneficiary. Such assets follow the principle of classification **according to the top-level beneficiary**, and are not classified by the custodial vehicle:
1. Sovereign wealth fund: the ultimate beneficiaries are all citizens, classified as public capital;
2. Commercial wealth-management products, private/public funds, private pensions, and other custodial products: the ultimate beneficial interests belong to private subjects, classified under private capital.
## 1.3 Two Fundamental Forms of Movement

Changes in the state of capital holding are effected through the following two categories of asset movement; however, appreciation or depreciation of capital is not directly generated by asset movement itself, but arises largely from price changes of the assets themselves during the holding phase.
### 1.3.1 Asset Allocation

**Asset allocation**: A complex process initiated proactively by a subject in pursuit of asset appreciation, in which the subject adjusts the combination of its own asset forms.

**Characteristics of asset allocation**: This process is highly complex, involving such activities as seeking target assets or counterparties, negotiation, decision-making, and contracting. The search and negotiation phases consume substantial time and communication costs, often requiring multiple rounds of back-and-forth. It is the primary means by which a subject pursues asset appreciation, but it only accomplishes adjustments to the structure of asset holdings; whether assets ultimately appreciate or depreciate depends on the subsequent price changes of the assets held.
### 1.3.2 Asset Transactions

**Asset transaction**: An exchange process of assets carried out between two or more subjects within the process of asset allocation, effecting the transfer of capital interests among different subjects.

**Characteristics of asset transactions**: Transactions involve participation by two or more subjects and take the form of mutual exchange of assets.

**Possessing dual economic properties**: Zero-sum transfer of existing stock: an increase in one party's capital corresponds to a decrease in another party's capital, with the total social capital unchanged. Non-zero-sum distribution of increments: redistribution among different subjects of newly created social value.
 
> **Note** [Conceptual distinction]: Asset allocation emphasizes adjustments to the portfolio of assets within a single subject; asset transactions emphasize the exchange of interests between different subjects. Most asset allocation is accompanied by asset transactions; but exceptions exist — for example, when a subject establishes a company by contributing funds and the funds transform into equity assets held, this constitutes asset allocation and does not involve a cross-subject exchange of assets.
## 1.4 Two Paths of Capital Appreciation

**Entity-based operating appreciation**: Reliance on production capacity, production, and supply chains to create incremental cash flow, bringing about changes in the total scale of a subject's capital; this belongs to social incremental creation. **Financial-spread-based appreciation**: Reliance on fluctuations in financial asset prices and asset transactions to bring about changes in the scale of a subject's capital; this belongs to zero-sum gaming plus repricing.
 
> **Note** [Conceptual distinction]: The two appreciation paths are not mutually exclusive and can coexist in real economic activity. Both paths are realized through asset allocation or asset transactions; capital cannot change in scale out of thin air.

## Sovereignty, Power, Wealth, and the Axiom of Labor Measurement
 
> **Note**: This section is an extension of the foundational conceptual definitions of the entire text, reusing the definitions in 1.1–1.4 without re-defining them.

**Sovereignty**: The top-level acting subject whose core foundational function is to sustain public safety and social order, and which possesses the capacity to command macro-level resource allocation. Different national institutional systems and enforcement mechanisms exhibit real-world variation; this text extracts the cross-national general abstract core.

**Power**: The top-level public-resource command capacity exclusively belonging to sovereignty, possessing long-term inflation-resistance attributes.
 
**Wealth**: The exchangeable, usable real goods and services produced by social labor; the common object jointly created and jointly pursued by the three endowments of power, right, and labor, and not belonging to the endowment category of the tripartite subjects.
 
**Social average labor efficiency**: The volume of wealth producible per unit of labor time, trending upward along with technological iteration and productivity upgrading.
 
**Social average labor cost per unit of wealth**: The social average labor time required to produce one unit of wealth, inversely proportional to social average labor efficiency, trending downward along with technological progress.
 
**Social average labor price**: Under population steady-state conditions, the real social average labor price is approximately a constant; the nominal labor price broadly trends upward in step with the long-term CPI trajectory, constituting the rigid steady-state boundary of residents' labor compensation.
# Chapter 2 The Era Differences, Scale Laws, and Leverage Amplification Mechanism of Capital Expansion

This chapter constitutes the core derivation layer of the entire text, yielding four inferences that support the subsequent analysis of capital expansion and labor price compression.
### Inference 1: Aggregate Tendency Toward Growth

Changes in overall social capital are positive. From a long-term historical perspective, the overall human-social economy has maintained an upward trajectory. Correspondingly, at the level of the whole society, the overall scale of capital tends toward growth.
 
> **Note**: This conclusion describes the statistical trend of society as a whole and does not guarantee that any individual subject achieves capital scale growth; at the individual level, capital depreciation occurs.
### Inference 2: Gain Correlated with Scale

Under social average conditions, the absolute scale of newly added capital is positively related to the scale of existing capital. Under social average conditions, setting aside differences in individual ability and opportunity: the absolute scale of new capital a subject can generate is positively related to its own existing capital scale. Even where individual operating ability varies in strength, this quantitative relationship still holds. Within the same social-average environment, subjects with larger existing capital scales correspondingly achieve higher absolute scales of new capital.
 
> **Note**: This inference is grounded in holistic analysis at the social average level, excluding individual extreme outliers, and focuses on regularities observable at the group level. Key logic: Inference 1 (that overall social capital tends toward growth) must hold first for Inference 2's scale–new-capital relationship to have practical significance; if overall capital change were negative, then the larger the capital scale, the larger the absolute scale of capital depreciation.
### Inference 3: Vehicle Iteration

Capital expansion is realized through asset vehicles; the available tools and conditions differ across eras, affecting the efficiency and model of capital expansion.

**Era dominated by physical assets**: Capital expansion relies on non-financial physical assets. Expansion depends primarily on the productive capacity corresponding to physical assets, generating sustained operating cash flow, thereby driving the subject's capital scale growth. The available means of realization are relatively singular; capital expansion is mostly accompanied by new plant construction, equipment additions, and capacity expansion; asset-form conversion and ownership adjustments are costly and slow, and the speed of capital expansion is constrained by physical production conditions.

**Era dominated by financial capital**: Financial assets become an important vehicular means of capital expansion. Compared with the physical-asset-dominated stage, this category of means exhibits three characteristics:
- greater diversity and multiplicity of means;
- more flexible conversion of asset forms and turnover of ownership;
- faster scale adjustment and transaction circulation.
 
> **Key conclusion**: It is not financial assets themselves that are expanding; rather, the capital of each subject uses financial asset vehicles to achieve its own relatively rapid scale growth. The rise in the volume of financial assets observed by society is merely an external manifestation of capital expansion.
### Inference 4: Bidirectional Leverage Amplification

Debt is a financial-contractual means available to capital. A subject that assumes debt creates leverage, enabling it to command asset vehicles beyond its own capital base, accelerating the speed of capital expansion and amplifying the scale–new-capital effect described in Inference 2. The debt scale that can be levered is based on the subject's own capital as its floor. The leverage multiple is jointly determined by own capital and debt. In an environment where overall social capital tends toward growth, higher leverage expands the total pool of mobilizable resources and raises the achievable absolute scale of new capital.
 
> **Note**: Leverage has a bidirectional symmetric effect. When the environment is favorable, it amplifies capital scale growth; when asset prices decline and asset operations cause capital erosion, it synchronously amplifies depreciation and rapidly erodes own capital. Lending also existed in the physical-asset era, but leverage scale was generally constrained by physical collateral; entering the financial-capital era, debt instruments became more diverse and higher levels of leverage became achievable. Leverage cannot generate own capital; it merely adds schedulable resources on top of the own-capital floor and does not overturn the fundamental relationship between capital scale and new capital. ## 3.3 A Limited Logical Inference Combined with Chapter 2's Inferences (an explanatory path only, not an empirical causal conclusion)

The above evidence demonstrates the objective phenomena of the expansion of total private capital and its simultaneous concentration toward top households. Combined with the four inferences of Chapter 2, a mechanistic inference can be made:
1. Long-term overall social capital tends toward growth, providing the external environment for scale growth of existing capital;
2. Under social average conditions, subjects with larger capital floors can obtain higher absolute scales of new capital;
3. In the financial-capital-dominated era, the vehicular means available to capital are richer and more flexible, improving expansion efficiency;
4. Debt leverage, based on own capital as its floor, further amplifies the scale gap in capital brought about by scale.
 
> **Boundary reminder**: This is only a logical inference. Taxation, institutions, technological change, and asset price cycles also affect wealth distribution; the data in this chapter cannot exclusively prove this mechanism to be the cause.
## 3.4 Chapter Summary

The empirical data reveal two layers of fact: first, the overall scale of private capital registered under household names has expanded persistently relative to GDP; second, private capital has concentrated one-way toward top households, with the capital-scale changes brought by assets to the bottom groups being nearly nil. This distribution pattern constitutes the real-world background of sustained downward pressure on labor prices.

# Chapter 4 Labor Price Compression: A Supply–Demand Perspective and U.S. Empirical Observation

This chapter independently develops the labor-side analysis, without restating the full derivations of Chapters 1 and 2, but may be contrasted with the capital-concentration phenomena of Chapters 2 and 3. Data sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), Economic Policy Institute (EPI), St. Louis Fed FRED database. Objective observational facts are distinguished from logical inference; the inference is only an explanatory path, not a causal conclusion.
## 4.1 Productivity Gains Bring About a Relative Expansion of Labor Supply

Modern technological iteration has driven sustained productivity gains across society. More products and services can be produced per unit of work time, which is equivalent to an expansion of the effective labor capacity society can provide. In physical production, automation, equipment upgrading, and technical improvements have reduced the labor hours required to accomplish output of equivalent scale. Against the background of rising productivity, society's effective labor supply is expanding, but the corresponding effective demand for jobs has not expanded in step; some areas have even seen a relative contraction in job demand. From the fundamental logic of market supply and demand: when effective labor supply increases while job demand does not expand proportionally, under factor-market conditions labor compensation faces downward pressure.

> **Note**: What is discussed here is relative supply–demand relations, not necessarily an absolute decline in employment numbers; total output is still growing, but the labor consumed per unit of output continues to decline, forming pressure from a relative surplus of labor supply.
## 4.2 Macro-Level U.S. Evidence: The Divergence Between Productivity and Labor Compensation

Selecting long-run data from the U.S. nonfarm business sector: from 1979 to 2019, net labor productivity in the U.S. nonfarm sector rose cumulatively by 59.7%; over the same period, the median hourly compensation of ordinary workers, adjusted for inflation, grew by only 15.8%, with the productivity growth rate roughly 3.5 times the wage-compensation growth rate — a gap appears between the two. The labor income share of national income likewise shows a long-run decline: in the post-war decades the labor income share remained within a 61%–66% range; entering the 21st century it kept falling, recently descending to a 53%–57% range, a post-war statistical low.

> **Observed fact**: while total economic output and labor productivity rise, the share of national output received by workers contracts, and the real compensation growth of ordinary workers lags the pace of productivity gains. This is the macro-level manifestation of labor price compression.

## 4.4 Contrasted Understanding with the Preceding Text (brief linkage, without repeating earlier text)

Chapter 3's data have shown private capital persistently concentrating toward a minority of households; this chapter, from the perspective of labor-factor market supply and demand, sees labor price compression. The two sets of phenomena reflect each other: total social wealth continues to expand, but at the distribution level, the share of distribution obtained by the capital factor rises while the share obtained by the labor factor declines.

But the boundary must be made clear: labor price compression is not fully determined by a single supply–demand mechanism. Union power, institutions, globalization, industrial policy, and bargaining position all affect wage levels; the supply–demand inference of this chapter is merely one logical path explaining labor compression, not an exclusive cause.
## 4.5 Chapter Summary

1. Rapid productivity gains expand society's effective labor supply; meanwhile, demand for labor per unit of output contracts relatively, creating a relative oversupply of the labor factor and constituting the foundational mechanism of labor price compression.
2. U.S. macro data show that in recent decades labor productivity has risen substantially while the real compensation growth of ordinary workers has lagged, and the labor income share of national income has continued to decline, reflecting the macro-level outcome of labor price compression.
3. The industry employment structure has migrated: the share of high-productivity manufacturing jobs has fallen persistently, and labor has shifted in large volume toward services, further amplifying supply pressure on part of the labor jobs.
4. The above supply–demand mechanism is only one explanatory dimension; real-world wage levels result from the joint operation of multiple economic and institutional factors.
# Chapter 5 The Capital–Labor Scissors Gap, Household Debt Accumulation, and the Sovereign-Debt Backstop Mechanism

This chapter follows the observed facts of Chapters 3 and 4: capital concentrates toward a minority, while labor prices remain persistently under pressure. Objective data observation is distinguished from logical inference; the inference is only an explanatory path, not causal. Data sources: Federal Reserve Z.1 Flow of Funds, St. Louis Fed FRED, U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA), U.S. Treasury, Congressional Budget Office (CBO).
## 5.1 The Formation of the Capital–Labor Scissors Gap

Chapter 3's evidence showed household private capital persistently concentrating toward top groups; Chapter 4 observed that labor compensation growth has long lagged productivity and that the labor share of national income has declined. Against the backdrop of continuous expansion of total social output, capital relies on its already-expanded capital floor, financial instruments, and leverage to drive ever-larger increments in the capital scale of subjects; meanwhile, ordinary workers' real wages grow weakly, and labor's share of national income keeps shrinking. This gives rise to a gap between capital-derived income and labor-derived income, which amplifies progressively over time.

The essence of the gap is not that total social wealth has stopped growing, but that the distribution structure of newly added value has shifted: a larger portion of new output flows to capital-holding subjects, while the share of distribution obtained by ordinary workers through labor is persistently squeezed.

> **Note**: The gap is an observed phenomenon at the distribution level; its formation results from the superimposition of multiple factors — capital scale effects, technological progress, industrial structure, and institutional environment — and capital concentration is only one driving dimension, not the whole explanation.
## 5.2 Transmission of the Gap: Rising Household Debt Levels and Intensified Living Pressure

When labor income growth fails to keep pace with the rise in social output, prices, and living costs, but households must maintain their existing consumption and standard of living, debt becomes an important buffer. For the vast majority of ordinary households lacking large-scale capital, the capital-scale change brought by assets is nearly negligible, and income depends mainly on labor compensation. Under conditions in which the labor share of distribution is squeezed:
1. Real disposable labor income growth of households is constrained;
2. Rigid living expenditures on housing, medical care, and education continue to rise;
3. To sustain necessary consumption and living standards, households resort to credit instruments to fill the gap in labor income, driving the expansion of household debt.

In addition, the Federal Reserve publishes the debt service ratio (DSR) — the share of debt principal-and-interest payments in disposable income (excluding mortgage principal repayment): near 15.5% at the 2007 peak; about 11.1% in 2024–2025, meaning households devote USD 11.1 of every USD 100 of disposable income to servicing debt principal and interest, creating persistent cash-flow consumption.

**Observational interpretation**: Household debt has long remained at a relatively high level — part arising from asset purchases and part used to fill the gap between labor income and living expenditures — so households continuously bear debt-servicing pressure and their overall living pressure is amplified. Bottom households, lacking the capital-scale change brought by assets, feel the constraint of debt burden especially acutely.

**Boundary note**: The rise in household debt also stems from other factors such as real estate prices, consumption culture, and the credit system; the gap's squeezing of labor income is the driving logic, not the sole cause.
## 5.3 Household Debt Constraints Emerge, and Sovereign Debt Passively Assumes a Social Backstop Function

Once household debt accumulates to a certain threshold, the scope for households to further increase leverage is constrained by income and debt-servicing capacity, and households can no longer rely on their own credit to offset distributional contradictions. If household debt contraction and declining consumption are allowed to proceed, aggregate demand will be suppressed and the economy will face the pressure of insufficient aggregate demand. To prevent the economy from falling into deep recession and to maintain the stability of macroeconomic operation, the sovereign level intervenes to provide a backstop.

The backstop mainly takes two paths: first, social-security transfer payments — through fiscal means such as social security, unemployment benefits, and various subsidies, transferring income to labor groups to offset the consumption gap caused by the decline in labor's share; second, countercyclical fiscal expansion — when private-sector consumption and investment weaken, the government expands fiscal spending to boost social aggregate demand and avoid a substantial collapse of aggregate demand.

When tax revenue is insufficient to cover expenditure, such fiscal behavior directly drives the expansion of sovereign debt.

Relevant U.S. government observational data.
1. Federal government debt scale:
- Federal debt was about 33% of GDP in 1980;
- about 54% in 2000;
- in 2024–2025, total federal debt exceeded USD 37 trillion, with debt-to-GDP reaching the 120%–127% range; publicly held debt was about 98% of GDP.

2. Scale of transfer payments for social backstopping (federal social-benefit transfers to individuals):
- In fiscal 2024, federal social-benefit transfers to individuals totaled about USD 3.32 trillion;
- in fiscal 2025, about USD 3.63 trillion; this portion accounts for roughly 11% of GDP, is a single largest federal fiscal spending block, includes social-security pensions, health care, and various relief subsidies, and assumes the social backstop function.

**Important note**: Federal debt cannot be equivalently attributed directly to social backstopping; federal debt also includes large amounts of other expenditure — defense, interest, infrastructure, tax cuts, crisis bailouts, and the like. It can only be said that social transfer-payment backstopping is a component that pushes up fiscal spending, widens the deficit, and thereby raises sovereign debt, rather than being the sole source.

**Key mechanism**: The capital–labor scissors gap squeezes residents' labor income, and the scope for households to increase leverage gradually peaks; at the macro level, to stabilize aggregate demand, sovereign fiscal policy expands passively, and sovereign debt rises accordingly. Sovereign debt in essence transfers part of the private sector's distributional contradiction onto the public sector's balance sheet.

**Important reminder**: The rise in sovereign debt results from the superimposition of multiple historical events — demographic structure, wars and crises, and tax-cut policies also play a role; the inference in this chapter only describes the transmission chain, not causation.
## 5.4 The Inherent Limits of the Transmission Chain

This operating model has inherent boundary constraints:
1. Household debt has an upper limit of debt-servicing capacity and cannot expand without bound; excessive household debt sows credit risk;
2. Sovereign debt likewise faces a market-absorption boundary: debt interest persistently consumes fiscal resources, and backstopping capacity has its limits;
3. Sovereign debt merely transfers and postpones the contradiction; it does not, at its root, change the underlying distribution structure between capital and labor.

As long as the capital–labor distributional gap persists, the distributional pressure of the private sector will continue to transmit outward, repeatedly testing the limits of household debt ceilings and sovereign fiscal backstopping capacity.
## 5.5 Chapter Summary

1. Under the joint effect of persistent capital concentration and labor price compression, an ever-widening scissors gap forms between capital-derived and labor-derived income, and the distribution of newly created social value tilts toward the capital end.
2. Ordinary households rely heavily on labor income, whose share of distribution is squeezed; data show that the U.S. household debt-to-disposable-income coefficient has risen persistently, driving up household debt to maintain living standards and intensifying household living and debt-servicing pressure.
3. When households reach the constraint on their leverage room, and to avoid the collapse of aggregate demand, the sovereign engages in macro backstopping through large-scale social transfer payments and fiscal expansion; the corresponding fiscal gap drives up sovereign debt levels. Social-benefit transfer payments have already become an enormous block of federal fiscal expenditure.
4. The rise in household debt and sovereign debt results from multiple superimposed factors; the transmission chain described in this chapter is only an explanatory path. Sovereign debt serves more to postpone and transfer the contradiction than to dissolve the underlying distributional contradiction.
# Chapter 6 Comparison of Two Backstop Paths: The U.S. Passive Sovereign-Debt Model and the Nordic Sovereign-Capital Hedging Model

This chapter follows the transmission chain of Chapter 5. In the modern financial-capital era, private capital concentration, the widening capital–labor scissors gap, and labor price compression are endogenous problems of this economic system, not unique to the United States. All economies adopting this economic operating model face this contradiction, but they may choose different institutional instruments to hedge and adjust. The United States belongs to the passive sovereign-debt backstop; the Nordic countries, with Norway as the representative, proactively construct public capital, hedging distributional contradictions in advance from the asset end.

Objective facts are distinguished from logical inference, and no value judgments are made. Data sources: U.S. Treasury, CBO; Norwegian Ministry of Finance, Norges Bank Investment Management (NBIM) Government Pension Fund Global (GPFG) public reports.
## 6.1 The Common Underlying Contradiction of the Modern Financial-Capital Era

Under the modern financial-capital operating framework, as long as the free accumulation and expansion of private capital is permitted, endogenous tendencies arise:
1. Private capital gradually concentrates toward a minority of households;
2. Productivity gains bring a relative expansion of labor supply, and labor prices face downward pressure;
3. A capital–labor scissors gap forms; ordinary households have little capital-scale change from assets, and the private sector readily develops an aggregate-demand gap.

This contradiction is endogenous to the system and is a problem common to all countries under this model, not a U.S. exception. The contradiction objectively exists, but this does not mean it will necessarily erupt violently; economies can use institutional instruments to intervene and hedge.
- United States: no large-scale public capital was built in advance. Only after the contradictions of the private sector had already accumulated and erupted did it initiate sovereign debt for post-hoc backstopping — a passive response.
- Nordic countries (Norway as the typical sample): the private capital market remains in operation, while they proactively build a large-scale public capital asset pool, hedging the distributional imbalance caused by private capital concentration from the capital-return end as the contradiction develops — proactive hedging.
 
> **Note**: The Nordic model does not eliminate private capital; domestic private enterprises and private equity investment remain active. Public capital is a public regulatory force superimposed on the market, not a wholesale replacement of the market economy.
## 6.2 Model One: The United States — the Passive Sovereign-Debt Backstop Model

### 6.2.1 Operating Mechanism: Passive Response, Post-Hoc Backstop

Private capital concentrates persistently and the labor income share is continually squeezed. Ordinary households first spontaneously rely on household leverage to fill the labor scissors gap; once household-sector debt reaches the debt-servicing ceiling and households can no longer increase leverage, private consumption begins to contract and the downside risk to aggregate demand emerges. The U.S. government holds no public capital assets of sufficient scale to draw upon and has no ready source of public capital returns. It can only, after the contradiction has already emerged, passively expand sovereign debt by issuing treasury bonds to raise funds. The fiscal funds are used for social-security transfer payments and countercyclical fiscal spending to offset the demand gap exposed by the private sector.

**Essential logic**: This is post-hoc passive backstopping. It expands the government's liability side, using future taxes as credit collateral to stabilize the present economy; this operation merely transfers and postpones the distributional contradiction without changing the underlying capital–labor distribution structure of the private sector.
### 6.2.2 Key Observed Data (continuing the earlier measures)

1. Total federal debt exceeded USD 37 trillion in 2024–2025, with total federal debt / GDP reaching 120%–127%; publicly held debt was about 98% of GDP.

2. Federal social-benefit transfers to individuals were about USD 3.32 trillion in 2024 and USD 3.63 trillion in 2025, about 11% of GDP, a single largest fiscal spending block.
### 6.2.3 The Inherent Constraints and Shortcomings of the Passive Model

1. Continuously generating an interest burden: treasury debt expansion brings year-on-year rising interest expense, which persistently squeezes current fiscal budgets.
2. The contradiction is only shifted in time: the underlying mechanism creating the gap is unchanged; the private sector's distributional pressure is merely transferred and deferred to the government balance sheet, and the root contradiction remains.
3. External hard constraints: the bond market's acceptance, inflationary pressure, and domestic partisan political contestation all set limits on issuance scale, bounding backstopping capacity.
4. Potential risk: as the interest burden keeps rising, it squeezes the fiscal space for welfare and industry; in extreme scenarios it can disturb sovereign creditworthiness.
 
> **Note**: The United States also has tax-adjustment instruments, but their adjustment power is limited; at the macro level it relies mainly on treasury expansion to accomplish post-crisis backstopping.
## 6.3 Model Two: The Nordic Countries (Norway as Representative) — the Proactive Sovereign-Capital Hedging Model

### 6.3.1 Operating Mechanism: Proactive Hedging, Relying on Public Asset Returns

Relying on fiscal primary surpluses from petroleum resources, Norway established the Government Pension Fund Global (GPFG) — that is, large-scale public capital. The government holds equity, bonds, real estate, and various financial assets globally on behalf of all citizens. Like private capital, public capital can continuously drive its own capital-scale change and obtain dividends and asset appreciation. It does not need to rely heavily on borrowing; instead, it uses the investment returns of public capital to supplement the fiscal budget and support social welfare and transfer payments. In this way, a substantial portion of the returns on society-wide capital-scale change accrues to the public and all citizens, offsetting the distributional imbalance caused by the concentration of private capital toward a minority of households and easing the capital–labor scissors gap.

**Key points**:
1. It does not eliminate private capital; private enterprises and private capital continue to participate in market operation;
2. It is proactive front-loading adjustment, not remedial action after the contradiction fully erupts; public capital, as a new subject, participates in sharing the returns brought by capital-scale change;
3. It converts part of the gains of capital-scale growth into universal welfare, easing the pressure on ordinary groups that rely solely on labor income.
### 6.3.2 Key Observed Data (Norwegian GPFG)

As of 2024–2025, the Norwegian sovereign fund GPFG held total assets of about USD 1.7 trillion; Norway's own GDP is about USD 500 billion, so the sovereign fund's scale is about 340% of its GDP.

Each year the fund transfers a set proportion of investment returns into the national budget for public welfare spending; in principle the principal is not touched, and only investment returns are consumed.

About 70% of the fund's assets are allocated to global equity assets, with the remainder in bonds and real estate, directly participating in global capital scale growth.
### 6.3.3 The Boundary Conditions of the Sovereign-Capital Model (not a panacea)

Initial accumulation requires resource dividends as a starting foundation, but resource endowment alone is insufficient to sustain long-run viability; **resource dividends combined with supporting institutional constraints are both indispensable, and the institutional framework is harder to replicate**. Without rigorous institutional design, resource returns are easily spent directly on current fiscal consumption or subject to interest tunneling, and the public capital floor will be quickly depleted. This institutional system includes the GPFG's ethical guidelines, a fully transparent governance framework, and fiscal-budget rules constraining annual withdrawal ratios, which mechanically safeguard the principal and permit use of investment returns only.

Public capital itself also faces market risk: stock market downturns and asset price drawdowns directly reduce the investment returns available for welfare.
Merely improving distributional outcomes does not completely eliminate the market's endogenous tendency toward capital concentration; coordination with other institutions such as taxation and social security is still required.

Governance difficulties exist: the chain of entrusted management, investment decisions, and information disclosure of enormous public assets is long; how to avoid rent-seeking by power and prevent interest tunneling, while continuously sustaining a transparent and credible governance system, is a long-term real-world challenge.

## 6.5 Chapter Summary

1. Under the modern financial-capital system, capital concentrating toward a private minority, labor price compression, and a widening capital–labor gap are latent problems endogenous to the system and common challenges faced by all countries under this model.
2. The United States did not build large-scale public public capital in advance; when household debt reached its ceiling and the aggregate-demand gap emerged, it could only passively rely on treasury issuance for post-hoc backstopping. This can stabilize the current macro economy but accumulates government debt pressure and merely postpones the contradiction.
3. The Nordic path represented by Norway uses resource dividends to build public capital, proactively hedging with the investment returns of public assets, sharing part of capital returns with all citizens and easing distributional contradictions; however, this model has high practical thresholds and cannot simply be replicated.
4. Neither model abolishes the private capital market; the two merely choose two different adjustment paths on the balance sheet — "post-hoc backstop on the liability side" versus "proactive hedge on the asset side" — each with its own applicable conditions and inherent limitations.
# Chapter 7 The Original-Principal Sources of Public Capital: Capital Gains Tax and National Natural-Resource Returns

This chapter follows the comparison of the two models in Chapter 6. Public capital differs from sovereign debt: debt relies on national credit to borrow; public capital is an asset floor that must have genuine principal inflows and cannot be created out of nothing. Without stable large-scale principal sources, public capital cannot be built up in scale and thus cannot effectively hedge the distributional contradiction arising from the spontaneous concentration of private capital. In reality there are two core original-principal channels: first, levying capital gains tax on private capital returns; second, resource returns generated by nationally owned natural resources. The two paths are not mutually exclusive and can be used in combination. Objective observed facts are distinguished from logical inference; no value judgments are made.
## 7.1 The Core Threshold of Public Capital: Where Does the Original Principal Come From

In the sovereign-debt model, the government can issue treasury bonds on the strength of sovereign credit and immediately obtain fiscal funds — "borrow first, repay with taxes later." The sovereign-capital model is entirely different: it must accumulate a large block of financial assets on the government's asset side, requiring sustained real monetary inflows as original principal; it cannot rely on debt issuance as the core source of principal.
 
> **Supplemental reminder**: In theory a government could also borrow money to invest, but that would be "assets bought with debt," simultaneously carrying corresponding debt; it is not pure public capital, carries an interest burden, and differs from the logic of sovereign funds like Norway's GPFG.

Without sustained principal injections, public capital can only slowly grow by relying on the investment returns of existing assets; the expansion speed is slow and it is hard to keep pace with the expansion pace of private capital.

Two feasible major principal sources in reality:
1. Extracting from the scale change of private capital: capital gains tax;
2. Treating the country's natural resources as capital owned in common by the whole people, and consolidating resource returns as public principal.
## 7.2 Path One: Capital Gains Tax — Extracting Public Principal from Private Capital Appreciation

### 7.2.1 Operating Mechanism

As Chapter 3's data have already shown, in the modern financial-capital era private capital continues to concentrate toward a minority of households, with top groups obtaining large amounts of dividends, equity appreciation, equity transfers, and other capital gains — this portion is capital-end capital-scale change, not entirely from labor. Capital gains tax is the taxation of private capital returns such as equity appreciation, dividends, and asset dispositions. The key institutional-design point distinguishing it from ordinary taxation: the revenue collected from capital gains tax is not fully used for current fiscal consumption or current transfer payments; a fixed proportion is not spent in the current period but is transferred into the public capital account as original principal for long-term outward investment.

**The complete logical chain**: private capital realizes capital gains → capital gains tax is levied → a portion of the tax revenue is retained, not consumed in current fiscal spending, and transferred into public capital as new principal → public capital participates in market investment, continuously generating dividends and asset appreciation → the investment returns of public capital are used for social welfare and redistribution, hedging the capital–labor gap caused by private capital concentration.
### 7.2.2 Advantages of the Path

1. The revenue source expands automatically with the capital cycle: when private capital prospers and assets surge, capital gains tax revenue rises correspondingly, allowing the public capital floor to be continuously enlarged;
2. The distributional logic corresponds directly to the root of the contradiction: directly extracting from the scale-change returns of private capital is equivalent to adjusting the spontaneous concentration of capital at its source;
3. It does not depend solely on natural-resource endowment; economies without resources can also follow this path, giving it wider applicability.
### 7.2.3 Real-World Constraints and Shortcomings

1. There is real resistance to collecting capital gains tax: capital can flow across borders and assets can be structured to avoid taxes, compressing the actual tax base;
2. If the tax rate is set too high, it may suppress the investment willingness of domestic private capital; the rate balance point needs weighing;
3. During economic downturns and bear markets, private capital gains shrink substantially and this tax source contracts markedly, slowing public capital principal injection;
4. Supporting rigorous institutions are needed to ensure this tax revenue is not diverted to current fiscal spending; once consumed by the current year's budget, it cannot form public capital principal.
## 7.3 Path Two: National Natural-Resource Returns — Treating Natural Resources as Capital Owned in Common by the Whole People

### 7.3.1 Operating Mechanism

Land, oil and gas, minerals, forests, and other natural resources are themselves a country's natural endowment, not created by private labor or private capital. Institutionally, core natural resources are defined as capital owned in common by all citizens. Through extraction, concession, franchising, resource taxes, resource concession fees, state-owned-enterprise dividends, and other means, natural resources are converted into monetary resource returns; this portion of returns does not go to a minority of private subjects but belongs to the sovereign public sector, and is continuously injected into the public capital account as original principal.

Norway is the most typical sample of this path: petroleum resources belong to the whole people, and the huge concession fees from oil and gas extraction and dividends from state equity in petroleum enterprises are mostly transferred into the Government Pension Fund Global, constituting the most important original principal of the sovereign fund.

**Logical chain**: Natural resources owned in common by the whole people (natural entity capital) → development converts them into resource rents, concession fees, and state-owned resource enterprise dividends → returns are consolidated into public capital as original principal → public capital invests outward and obtains capital returns → investment returns are used for social redistribution for all citizens, easing the capital–labor scissors gap.

**Conceptual point**: Here natural resources are themselves treated as original capital owned in common by citizens; they exist without prior human capital investment; the rent returns generated after development are the cash flow brought by this public capital.
### 7.3.2 Advantages of the Path

1. Resource returns are a "natural-endowment dividend," not taken by taxation from private parties, so social resistance is relatively smaller;
2. If resource reserves are large and market prices are favorable, huge principal can be injected into public capital in a relatively short time, rapidly enlarging the public asset floor;
3. Resource returns are independent of ordinary income tax and capital gains tax and can be combined with capital gains tax to doubly enrich public capital.
### 7.3.3 Real-World Constraints and Shortcomings

1. Strong endowment dependence: this path depends heavily on the country's natural-resource reserves and market prices. Economies lacking large-scale high-value natural resources find it hard to rely on this path alone;
2. Strong cyclical volatility of commodity prices: during downturns in oil, gas, and mineral prices, resource returns shrink substantially and principal injection decreases accordingly;
3. High governance risk: resource extraction and concession authorization easily breed interest tunneling and corruption; state ownership of resources does not automatically mean returns become the whole people's public capital — rigorous legal institutions are needed to ensure returns are paid up and to prevent partial retention;
4. Resource development also entails environmental, industrial-structure, and other external problems.
 
> **Extended discussion: an empirical counterpoint to institutional isolation**
> 
> During the rapid real-estate expansion of the past few decades, local governments relied on land-transfer revenue to form a de facto "resource-dividend-like" window, sedimenting enormous land and real-estate assets. But land-transfer cash flow was not consolidated and isolated; it was used directly for current infrastructure, local operations, and debt servicing — a "spend each incoming sum as it arrives" flow-consumption model, with no principal-lockup mechanism and without restricting withdrawals to investment returns. After the dividend cycle ended, no sustainable sovereign-capital floor generating public returns was formed.
> 
> This contrasts with the GPFG model: petroleum dividends are all channeled into the fund's principal pool, and the fiscal authorities may only withdraw a limited proportion of investment returns, with the principal pool not to be touched. The two cases jointly prove: the dividend window is only a necessary condition; establishing the institutions of cash-flow consolidation, principal preservation, and rigid withdrawal constraints during the dividend window is the critical watershed. The dividend window is not sustainable; once the window period is missed, **the cost and institutional resistance of rebuilding a public capital floor of equivalent scale rise significantly**.
## 7.4 The Combinability of the Two Paths

The two sources are not a choice of one or the other; they can be combined:
1. Resource dividends as the mainstay, capital gains tax as a supplement: countries rich in natural resources rely on resource returns as the primary principal source, supplemented by moderate capital gains tax to further enlarge public capital;
2. Without large-scale natural resources: mainly relying on a reasonably designed capital gains tax system, continuously extracting part of private capital scale change to slowly accumulate public capital principal.
 
> **Important reminder**: Regardless of the source, institutional isolation mechanisms are required (see 7.2.3, item 4); consolidated principal in principle must not be freely diverted to ordinary current fiscal spending.
## 7.5 Chapter Summary

1. Public capital and sovereign debt differ in essence: sovereign debt relies on credit to borrow money; public capital requires sustained original-principal inflows and is the hard precondition for establishing a public asset floor.
2. The capital gains tax path: extracting a portion from the scale-change returns of private capital, not for current consumption, and transferring it as public capital principal. Its advantage is that it does not depend on resource endowment and directly targets the source of capital concentration; its disadvantage lies in practical difficulties such as capital-mobility tax avoidance, cyclical fluctuation, and institutional constraints.
3. The national natural-resource return path: defining natural resources as entity capital owned in common by the whole people, and converting resource rents, concession fees, and state-owned resource enterprise dividends into public capital original principal. Norway's sovereign fund is the typical case of this path; but it is strongly constrained by resource endowment, commodity cycles, and governance capacity.
4. The two paths can be combined, but both require sound legal institutions to prevent principal from being consumed by current fiscal spending, so that a public capital floor capable of hedging distributional contradictions can truly be precipitated.
# Chapter 8 The United States: The Closing of Structural Windows and the Path Predicament

This chapter follows Chapters 6 and 7. The preceding text laid out the endogenous contradictions of the modern financial-capital system and provided two feasible original-principal paths for building public capital: national natural-resource returns and directed allocation of capital gains tax. In its historical development, the United States has gradually missed the time windows through which these two instruments could smoothly hedge the contradiction of private capital concentration, and can only continue to rely on sovereign debt for passive backstopping. Objective facts are distinguished from logical inference; this chapter constitutes a trend inference, not a prediction of necessarily occurring future events. Data sources: U.S. Treasury, Congressional Budget Office (CBO), U.S. Department of the Interior (BLM), U.S. Census Bureau, CDC-NCHS, OECD health statistics reports.
## 8.1 The Two Historical Windows for Building Public Capital Are Essentially Closed

The two core principal sources for building public capital — national natural-resource returns and directed allocation of capital gains tax — have, from institutional and real-world conditions, essentially closed their early historical windows in the United States.

**First**, the window for natural resources as public principal has disappeared. Through the Mining Act, the Homestead Acts, and other measures, the United States early on transferred large swaths of federal public land and underground mineral resources to private subjects, with the property rights of substantial oil, gas, and mineral resources vested in private enterprises and private landowners. Although some federally owned land and minerals remain in the West, much high-economic-value resources have completed privatization of title; the few federal oil-and-gas reserves in history have also been privatized and disposed of, such as the outright sale of the Elk Hills Naval Petroleum Reserve. To this day, the United States has no large-scale incremental natural-resource dividends directly collectable by the state. Unlike Norway, which established the institution of whole-people ownership of oil-and-gas resources before large-scale petroleum development, the U.S. resource property-rights structure has already hardened, making it hard to generate enormous, sustained public returns through natural-resource development as public capital original principal. Even though the federal government still holds some land and minerals, it can only collect limited lease-use fees, the scale of which is insufficient to support a public asset floor adequate to hedge the country's total private capital scale.

**Second**, the institutional window for directed allocation of capital gains tax to enrich public capital has also lost its real-world political foundation. In theory the United States could adjust its capital gains tax system, setting aside a portion of tax revenue from current fiscal consumption and transferring it into a public capital account. But in reality multiple rigid constraints exist: first, under the existing tax system, capital gains have numerous deductions, exemptions, and cross-border tax-avoidance channels, with a low actual effective rate, and high-net-worth groups possess abundant structuring means to compress the tax base; second, raising capital gains tax and establishing a dedicated public capital account require legislation through both houses of Congress and face strong resistance from capital interest groups and partisan contestation — most such proposals historically have failed to land, and the policy direction has even tended toward further reducing the capital gains tax burden; third, the federal budget has long been in deficit, with annual fiscal revenue insufficient to cover expenditure, and all tax revenue is prioritized for rigid current spending on social security, health care, defense, and interest. Even if capital gains tax were raised modestly, the incremental revenue would be directly used to fill the current year's fiscal gap, making it hard to retain and precipitate as long-term, untouchable public capital principal.

In sum, the United States has neither large-scale monetizable national natural-resource dividends, nor can it, under the existing political landscape, convert capital gains into sustained public principal. The two main roads to smoothly building public capital and proactively hedging the contradiction of private capital concentration are, under real-world conditions, very difficult to walk through.
## 8.2 It Can Only Continue to Rely on Sovereign Debt Backstopping; Debt Enters a Self-Reinforcing Expansion Cycle, and the Real Living Pressure of Residents Becomes Fully Entrenched

When the two proactive hedging paths of public capital fail, the United States can only continue to use the passive model described in Chapter 5: relying on expanding sovereign debt to absorb the aggregate-demand gap caused by the private sector's distributional contradiction. This model forms a self-reinforcing cycle.

Private capital persistently concentrates → the capital–labor scissors gap widens → household debt reaches its ceiling → the government issues debt for social backstopping → debt scale expands and interest expense rises rapidly → interest in turn forces the government to issue new debt to cover part of the debt-servicing cost, further inflating debt.

Observed real-world data: as of Q2 2026, total U.S. federal debt exceeded USD 40 trillion, with debt-to-GDP above 125%; annual net interest expense exceeded USD 1 trillion, and interest has become one of the faster-growing rigid expenses in the federal budget, with interest expense already exceeding the defense budget. A large share of fiscal revenue is used directly for debt service rather than industrial investment or welfare improvement. Under the CBO baseline forecast, absent major reform, the debt-to-GDP ratio will continue to rise over the coming decades, exhibiting near-exponential expansion.

This debt expansion cycle simultaneously harbors the symmetric risk of leverage. In asset up-cycles, leverage symmetrically amplifies the wealth gains of both types of subjects; once the economy enters an asset-price down-cycle, the depreciation-amplifying effect of leverage simultaneously strikes the look-through head private capital behind corporate vehicles and the debt-laden household sector.

Head private capital, relying on corporate vehicles, holds enormous financial assets with leverage. When asset prices correct, asset shrinkage is amplified by the leverage multiple; the impact first manifests at the corporate level and then transmits, through channels such as equity, dividends, asset dispositions, and taxation, to the top-level private subjects; corporate vehicles and fiscal-tax mechanisms provide a certain buffer, deferring the realization of losses.

The household sector, meanwhile, relies on mortgages and consumer credit to hold assets with leverage; its debt-servicing obligations are rigid and lack comparable buffering mechanisms. In asset-down phases it readily enters a negative-equity state in which asset values fall below the remaining debt, and household net wealth contracts rapidly.

The leverage risks of the two are symmetric, but their capacity to withstand shocks is markedly asymmetric. This risk is the secondary contradiction hidden within the asset-price cycle under this sovereign-debt backstop model.
### 8.2.1 Income "Averaging Up": The Severe Disconnect Between Book High Income and the Real Cash Flow of Ordinary People

U.S. published statistics on average household income exhibit a strong top-end pull effect and cannot reflect the real living conditions of ordinary people. According to official U.S. Census Bureau ACS data: the arithmetic average annual household income in the United States is about USD 113,000, but the median household income is only about USD 87,000 — a gap of nearly USD 26,000.

The arithmetic mean is substantially inflated by top-tier high-capital-scale-change returns and high-compensation groups, creating a book illusion of "universal high income." More than half of ordinary households and the bottom labor groups in society have real income levels significantly below the average figures touted in public discourse. In a U.S. economic structure where rigid expenditures on housing, medical care, insurance, and education remain high year-round, ordinary households lack ample surplus and have extremely weak risk-bearing capacity — this is the underlying real reason the household sector must long rely on credit and sustain high-debt living.
### 8.2.2 World-Class Health Care, Yet Life Expectancy Declines Against the Trend, Corroborating the Survival Pressure of the Whole Population

The United States possesses world-class medical technology, relatively high per-capita medical spending, and a comprehensive medical-hardware system. By conventional development logic, its population-health and longevity indicators should long lead developed economies. But the actual statistics present a completely contrary structural paradox: the United States is the only high-income, major developed economy in which life expectancy has persistently declined.

CDC and NCHS official data: U.S. life expectancy at birth was 78.8 years in 2019; it fell to 76.4 years in 2021, directly receding to the level of the mid-to-late 1990s — a structural collapse.

Over the same period, all OECD developed countries achieved recovery and rebound in longevity indicators; only the United States kept declining.

The study *Deaths of Despair* by Nobel laureates Case and Deaton points out that, among non-Hispanic white labor groups, the three categories of "deaths of despair" — opioid abuse, suicide, and alcoholic liver disease — are highly correlated with economic anxiety and social estrangement arising from long-term employment-opportunity shrinkage, stagnant income, and declining social status. The decline in life expectancy results from the superimposition of multiple factors — drug abuse, deficiencies in public-health governance, and social-safety problems are all important causes, and **the phenomenon must not be attributed to distributional imbalance alone**. But under the premise of globally best medical hardware and relatively high medical spending, the phenomenon of national longevity declining against the trend, combined with the empirical findings of *Deaths of Despair*, can corroborate from the side that long-term financial anxiety, high household debt pressure, stagnant labor income growth, and the tightness of bottom-tier living have deeply affected the quality of life and health of ordinary people. This is social corroboration of the entrenched capital–labor distributional imbalance and household living pressure.
## 8.3 The Predicament: Narrowing Reform Space and Structural Constraints

In the historical phase when the contradictions were still relatively mild, distributional pressure could be gradually relieved through modest tax adjustments and partial institutional reform. But as contradictions have accumulated over decades, with debt, wealth polarization, declining labor income share, and household survival pressure piling up on multiple fronts, the space for moderate, gradual reform has been continuously compressed.
- If the existing institutional path is maintained: private capital continues to concentrate, the labor income share remains under pressure, the debt pressure of households and government continues to rise, and the interest burden continuously erodes fiscal capacity; the various social problems corresponding to household economic distress will also continue.
- If an adjustment of greater force is attempted: whether regulating existing private capital or re-collecting a large-scale public asset floor, it will touch the already-hardened structure of vast private capital interests and require breaking through multiple constraints of the existing legal, property-rights, and political systems.

Within the existing institutional framework, there is a lack of smooth, gradual instruments to break through this structural predicament on a large scale. In other words, piecemeal repairs can hardly reverse a trend that has already taken shape.

> **It should be made clear**: this text's inference is not a prediction that the United States will inevitably erupt into some specific crisis, but rather points out a set of structural constraints: the historical window for proactive hedging has closed; the passive backstop model carries its own self-amplifying debt side effect; the feasible space for gradual improvement continues to narrow; and the survival pressure from high household debt and compressed labor compensation on the resident side will continue to transmit outward into various social outcomes.
## 8.4 Chapter Summary

1. There are two major real-world principal sources for building public capital: national natural-resource returns and directed allocation of capital gains tax. In its historical process, most high-value natural resources in the United States have completed privatization of title; meanwhile, constrained by the tax system, the interest structure, and long-term fiscal deficits, it is difficult to convert capital gains tax into sustained public principal. The windows for the two main paths to proactively hedge private capital concentration have essentially closed.
2. The United States can only continue to rely on sovereign debt for post-hoc passive backstopping, thereby forming a self-reinforcing cycle of "distributional contradiction — debt issuance for backstopping — rising interest — further debt expansion." Federal debt scale and interest burden have risen rapidly, debt exhibits accelerating expansion, and interest has become an enormous rigid consumption of the fiscal system. Beyond sustained debt expansion, this model also harbors the symmetric risk of leverage: in asset-down cycles it simultaneously strikes the look-through head private capital behind corporate vehicles and the highly indebted household sector, with markedly asymmetric capacities of the two types of subjects to withstand shocks.
3. American society exhibits income distortion: top-tier wealth inflates the overall mean, while the real income and cash-flow levels of ordinary households are far below book data; long-term rigid expenditures squeeze household surplus, forcing people to live on credit, with high debt becoming the norm.
4. Under the premise of world-class medical conditions, U.S. life expectancy declines against the trend, a phenomenon unique among developed economies. This indicator indirectly demonstrates that distributional imbalance, labor compression, household financial tightness, and chronic living anxiety have formed systematic, persistent survival pressure on the population.
5. As wealth polarization, debt pressure, and livelihood pressure continue to accumulate, the operational space for moderate, gradual reform is continuously compressed. To adjust this structural contradiction at its root would require breaking through the already-hardened property-rights and political interest structure; ordinary piecemeal repairs can hardly reverse the long-term trend.
6. This chapter is a structural-logical inference, not a deterministic prediction of a specific crisis or social upheaval; it is used only to compare the real-world feasibility boundaries of the two models in Chapters 6 and 8.
