# H10.5 Institutional Separation of the Federal Reserve: A Structural Shift in the Global Financial System

This lightweight working paper constructs a structural model to analyze long-run power dynamics between the U.S. federal government and the Federal Reserve, as well as the iterative evolution of the global dollar-based financial system.

The model delineates five sequential evolutionary phases: 
## **Phase 1**: Cooperative Regime. 
Prior to 2008, the U.S. government and the Federal Reserve operated under a mutually supportive institutional arrangement. The Treasury issued sovereign debt to global investors, forming a reciprocal capital circulation mechanism that sustained outward systemic prosperity. 

## **Phase 2**: Institutional Divergence. 
The 2008 global financial crisis exposed structural fiscal vulnerabilities arising from speculative asset bubbles and overseas fiscal commitments. Global investors became less willing to absorb incremental U.S. Treasury securities. The government’s financing gap expanded, forcing fiscal reliance on the Federal Reserve, which triggered fundamental divergence between the two institutions. 

## **Phase 3**: Separation Negotiation. 
In the post-crisis era, fiscal deficits persisted. The Federal Reserve began clarifying boundaries of institutional authority, while exploring independent institutional space and establishing cross-border institutional partnerships. This remains an endogenous bargaining process between the two domestic entities, without external intervention. 

## **Phase 4**: Partial Decoupling with Retained Linkages. 
While the original Treasury-bond anchored linkage weakens, the Federal Reserve establishes parallel liquidity facilities with foreign central banks. It explores alternative credit anchors and international alliances, pursuing institutional autonomy without fully severing existing connections.

### Actor Definition

**Federal Reserve**: An independent institutional actor backed by massive global financial asset stock. It is not subordinate to the U.S. government and can engage in negotiations with major global economic entities on an equal footing. 

**U.S. Government**: Possesses dominant geopolitical power yet bears heavy sovereign debt burden. Its primary objective is to restore the prior joint institutional regime. 

**Other major economies**: System participants and observers. They aim to mitigate risks from single-currency dependency, develop diversified cooperative channels, and participate in new multilateral institutional construction, while avoiding direct high-cost confrontation with the United States.

### The Fed’s Five-Step Progressive Strategy

1. **Liquidity arrangement**: Establish bilateral currency swaps and temporary liquidity facilities with partner central banks. 

2. **Anchor exploration**: Evaluate the feasibility of commodity-backed anchors and gold revaluation. 

3. **Reserve accumulation**: Increase holdings of gold and physical resource-backed assets. 

4. **Infrastructure development**: Construct cross-coalition trading and collaborative platforms. 

5. **Full deployment**: Formal operation of the multi-anchor credit system.

### Response Strategies of the U.S. Government

1. **Regime restoration**: Maintain the prior institutional framework and sustain fiscal overdraft supported by central bank balance sheet expansion. 

2. **External obstruction**: Interfere with the Federal Reserve’s cross-border partnerships and new institutional construction. 

3. **Coercive escalation**: Deploy coercive instruments if offshore institutional cooperation advances.
### Response Strategies of Other Major Economies

1. Welcome bilateral institutional cooperation with the Federal Reserve. 

2. Respond to U.S. obstruction with restrained and measured countermeasures. 

3. Adopt differentiated, robust pushback against U.S. coercive actions to safeguard cooperative entitlements.

## Summary of Evolutionary Trajectory

This transformation unfolds gradually over an extended time horizon, rather than via abrupt institutional rupture. The Federal Reserve avoids pushing the U.S. government into systemic collapse; it allocates partial benefits to mitigate disruptive fiscal incentives. Its core objective is institutional autonomy from fiscal capture. This bargaining equilibrium lays the foundation for the fifth-stage systemic configuration.

## Phase 5: Global Multilateral Co-Governance. 
After long-term iterative evolution, the Federal Reserve transforms into a neutral global public financial platform, decoupled from U.S. fiscal dominance. 

This is not a forced rupture, but a proactive institutional design. To secure systemic stability and constrain disruptive domestic fiscal behavior, the Federal Reserve preserves special procedural privileges for the United States, including priority settlement rights, veto buffers on major matters, and weighted representation. Other major economies obtain representation and authority commensurate with their economic capacity; detailed institutional rules will be finalized through multilateral negotiation.

Three binding constraints define America’s special status:

1. Termination of unlimited fiscal overdraft. Under the revised framework, global economies hold discretionary choice to refrain from purchasing U.S. sovereign debt.
2. Prohibition of unilateral rule violation. If the U.S. breaches multilateral rules, it will incur liquidity costs and associated penalties.
3. End of exclusive systemic dominance. The United States becomes one privileged participant rather than the sole system controller.

Final systemic configuration: The Federal Reserve serves as neutral coordinator; the United States retains limited special privileges; the system operates under global multilateral co-governance.
