# H13. Iteration of Civilizational Subjectivity: The Origin, Boundary and Progression of Fundamental Rights

## Abstract

This paper constitutes the fourth-stage deduction in the H-Series Civilizational Topology research. Following the research lineage of H10’s analysis of financial phenomena, H11’s binary axiom of wealth and rights, and H12’s evolution of institutional paradigms, this work shifts perspective toward human subjectivity. It places human subjectivity as the primary premise of civilization and distinguishes two categories: innate fundamental rights and socially derived rights. Among them, the right to subsistence and the right to knowledge, as innate fundamental rights, resist arbitrary domination by social rights and sit outside the wealth-right conversion framework defined in H12. This paper also explores an evolutionary logic: innate fundamental rights are not static; their boundaries expand gradually alongside civilizational paradigm shifts. This framework introduces human-centered constraints behind institutional paradigms, mitigating risks that rule systems erode individuals’ inherent rights, and refining the deductive framework for civilizational evolution.

## II. Civilizational Axiom: The Primacy of Subjectivity

Nearly all institutions, orders, rules, economies, finance and governance within human civilization function mostly as tools rather than ends.

The core orientation of civilizational evolution: to preserve, unlock and expand human subjectivity.

Whether examining the traditional identity-centric paradigm or the prospective voucher-metered paradigm; whether looking at personalized power structures or standardized rule-based orders, all systemic iterations, paradigm transformations and reconstructions of wealth-right order ought not to override human subjectivity.

A hierarchical precept is established for the entire H-Series framework: Subject prevails over rules; humanity prevails over institutions; origin prevails over architecture.

One limitation found in conventional political science and human rights theory: rights are often framed as grants from society, legislation or the state. As a result, rights tend to shift amid institutional revisions, power pressures and changing times.

This paper adjusts the underlying logic: human subjectivity may be regarded as a precondition for civilization’s operation, not merely a social product. Institutions may iterate, paradigms may shift, rules may be restructured, while the positioning of subjectivity as the primary premise remains stable.

## III. Bifurcation of Rights: Innate Fundamental Rights vs. Socially Derived Rights

Building upon the wealth-right bidirectional conversion system in H12, this paper draws a critical political-economic boundary division to reduce risks of systemic alienation and institutional pushback against subjects.

### 1. Innate Fundamental Rights (the foundational bedrock of subjectivity)

Definition: Inherent rights rooted in human subjectivity, formed prior to specific societies, institutions and rules.

Core attributes: Resistant to arbitrary domination, transaction, conversion, deprivation, quantification or offset. They are moderately insulated from identity, wealth, social authority and paradigm systems. Such rights sit outside the H12 wealth-right conversion channel. They are excluded from social metering systems, voucher pools and the adjustment of social rules.

This paper focuses on two innate fundamental rights:

- Right to subsistence: the baseline for individual existence
- Right to knowledge: the baseline for individual cognitive advancement and the growth of subjectivity

As societies evolve, institutions transform and resources reorganize: no individual, organization or rule system ought to arbitrarily interfere with another person’s subsistence and access to knowledge.

### 2. Socially Derived Rights (scope of governance in H12)

Definition: Acquired institutional rights formed in the course of social operation, public governance and resource allocation.

Examples include rights over resource scheduling, governance participation, public deliberation, and social quota allocation.

Attributes: Granted by rules, evolving with paradigms; capable of standardization, constraint, bidirectional conversion and voucher-based metering.

#### Core Boundary

Socially derived rights may be restructured by rules and quantified for circulation; innate fundamental rights need to remain detached from this social system.

A notable tension in traditional societies: identity-based power could suppress subsistence, monopolize knowledge and shrink the space for individual subjectivity. Future systems require safeguards to prevent quantification, wealth and governance systems from eroding people’s inherent rights.

## IV. Core Theorem: Progressive Advancement of Fundamental Rights alongside Civilizational Paradigms

Human innate fundamental rights are not static; their boundaries expand gradually with civilizational iteration.

With each paradigm transition of civilization, fundamental rights undergo expansion, enlargement and upgrading.

### 1. Forms of Rights in the Identity-Centric Era

- Right to subsistence: dependent on social strata, identity and external supply; public safeguards were weak
- Right to knowledge: largely monopolized by social strata, blocked by identity barriers
- Fundamental rights: low stability, vulnerable to external interference

### 2. Elevation of Rights under the Voucher-Metered Paradigm

After the paradigm becomes de-personalized, rules grow more equitable, and wealth-right order becomes standardized:

- Right to subsistence shifts from contingent support → baseline public safeguards. It relies less on personal connections, social strata or discretionary patronage, and rests upon basic safeguards built by public rule systems.
- Right to knowledge shifts from elite privilege tied to identity → universal rights for subjects. Knowledge is no longer confined to small privileged groups and becomes a civilizational resource accessible to more subjects on equal footing.
- Fundamental rights evolve from passive survival → support for the expansion of subjectivity.

### 3. Underlying Logic of Civilizational Progression

An important metric of civilizational advancement: institutional constraints on individuals gradually weaken, the boundaries of innate fundamental rights widen, and the freedom of human subjectivity rises.

As civilization continues to iterate, fundamental rights keep expanding, refining, elevating and generating new types of rights. Newly established foundational rights follow one consistent criterion: once consolidated as innate fundamental human rights, they move outside the scope of routine social games, wealth-right conversion and power domination.
